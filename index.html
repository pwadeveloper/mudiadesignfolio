    KayKav Academy LMS

    Part 1/11

    EdTech · Learning Platform

    Product Design & Build Direction

    Kaduna, Nigeria
    --:-- (+1 WAT)

    KayKav Academy is my school for creatives. Its learning platform is where students buy a course, learn through video, reading and quizzes, submit a final project and earn a certificate anyone can verify. The academy's team runs everything from the same app: courses, payments, students, invites, discounts, project reviews and certificates.

    I designed the product end to end and directed its build with an AI coding agent, from July to October 2026. This page is about what the platform does for the people who use it. How it was built is in the technical notes at the end.

    0Ways in: visitor, student and admin
    0Courses on the platform, including Built, Not Prompted
    0Admin areas, from course editor to certificates
    0Of each video actually watched before it counts
    0Expiry dates. Every purchase is lifetime access

    /01

    The Brief

    A calm reading room that happens to have excellent video

    KayKav Academy runs courses like Built, Not Prompted, which teaches non-developers to ship working software with AI tools. I had been teaching as live cohorts. To sell courses on their own I needed a platform that took payment in naira, kept paid video behind a real lock, and gave students a certificate that meant they had done the work.

    Off-the-shelf course tools fell short on two counts. Most can't take local payments well, and most treat a course as a video playlist. I wanted the reverse. The reference points were Domestika for craft video and the Interaction Design Foundation for structured, text-heavy rigour.

    The platform must feel like a calm reading environment that happens to have excellent video.From the product requirements

    /02

    Who It Serves

    One product, three ways in

    The same app serves three kinds of people, and each sees only what is theirs. A visitor can look around freely, a student sees the courses they hold, and an admin runs the academy.

    /03

    Student Side

    From first look to a certificate

    The student journey runs in seven steps, from browsing the catalogue to a certificate with its own public page. Each one was designed to answer the question a student has at that moment, and nothing else.

    /04

    Admin Side

    Running the academy from one place

    The admin side is where courses get written, payments get matched and students get looked after. It is eight areas in one sidebar, each built around a single job, with the next action always in the same place.

    /05

    Fair By Design

    Promises the product keeps for students

    A certificate only means something if it can't be faked, and a purchase only feels safe if nothing can go missing. These are the promises the product makes, and they hold whatever a browser claims.

    • Progress is earned by watching. A video counts once 90% of it has really been played. Skipping to the end moves your place, not your progress.Like a taxi meter: it charges for the minutes the ride took, whatever the passenger says.
    • Access opens when the payment is confirmed. A "payment successful" page isn't proof. Access waits for the payment provider's own confirmation, and arrives even if the buyer closes the tab.Like waiting for the bank alert before releasing goods, instead of trusting a screenshot of a transfer.
    • Answers stay hidden until you pass. The correct options never reach the student's browser before a pass.Like printing the exam paper without the answer key stapled to the back.
    • Anyone can check a certificate. Every certificate has a serial and a public page that says whether it is valid.Like a car's registration number: anyone can look it up, and it says exactly one thing.
    • The refund window is shown before paying. It sits beside the buy button, in plain words, not behind a link to the terms.

    /06

    Design

    Two looks, one set of roles

    Components are named by the job they do, like surface, ink and action, never by a colour. That let the product offer two looks from one set of parts: the marketing site's brand-blue canvas, or blue chrome around white reading surfaces for long sessions. Gold is kept for the one action on each screen, with a black label.

    The video player was rebuilt with 44px gold control tiles and a quality menu, and it resumes where you stopped. The reading column stays exactly 720px wide at every screen size, with the lesson outline beside it.

    /07

    Outcome

    Live, selling, and teaching

    The platform runs at learn.kaykav.academy and hosts the academy's courses, including Built, Not Prompted, the Druids Alpha Masterclass and Building a Better Creative Business. Students pay in naira through Paystack or by bank transfer, enrol by invite, take quizzes, submit projects and download certificates. The terms and privacy policy were approved by counsel and are published on the site.

    The bigger result for me is the method. A designer can direct an agent to build software that handles real money, as long as the work is broken into steps that prove themselves and every failure becomes a rule the next step has to read. That is what I now teach in Built, Not Prompted.

    Before

    • Courses taught only as live cohorts
    • No home for recordings, quizzes or certificates
    • Enrolment handled one student at a time

    After

    • Self-paced courses on the academy's own platform, live since August 2026
    • Video, reading, quizzes, projects and verifiable certificates in one place
    • Whole cohorts invited in one paste

    /T1

    The Method

    One verified step at a time, with a continuity log

    I don't write production code by hand. I design, specify and direct, and an AI agent writes the code. The risk with that setup is drift: step ten quietly builds on something the agent invented in step three. So I broke the build into a Prompt Pack of 24 steps, each with the same four parts.

    • Context. What's true now, and which spec governs this step.
    • Build. Exactly what to produce.
    • Verify. A command or test that proves it worked. If it fails, the next step doesn't start.
    • Handoff. What the next step needs to know, written into the build log in the same commit.

    The build log works like a script supervisor's continuity notes on a film set. Every take is logged, so whoever shoots tomorrow can match what was shot today. Any session, human or agent, starts by reading it, and each incident becomes a standing rule in the agent's instructions.

    0Lines in the build log

    194s→11sTest suite, after moving to a local database

    Stack Next.js · TypeScript · Supabase (Frankfurt) · Cloudflare Stream · Paystack · Tiptap · Tailwind · Vitest and Playwright

    /T2

    What Broke

    The incident log

    Agent-built software fails in specific ways, and I wanted a record of them more than a clean story. These are ten entries from the log, rewritten for readers outside the codebase. None cost anyone money or access.

    /T3

    What I Keep

    Six rules the log earned

    Most incidents shared one shape: a check that existed somewhere my gates didn't look. The fixes that held were tests that go and ask, written against a whole folder instead of the one file someone already suspected.

    • A comment is not a gate. A note saying "remember to add this" is only read by someone already in the file they forgot to open.From the admin navigation bug, twice
    • Passing tests don't mean it compiles. 916 tests once passed over a file that didn't compile. Types, lint and tests are three separate checks, run last.From 14.08.26
    • Test with the data that's meant to stay hidden. A record a user isn't allowed to see comes back as an empty result, not an error. Only a test that includes one can catch it.From the same silent bug in three features
    • Ship a refusal with its caller. Purely additive changes can go first. Anything that refuses or constrains ships with the code that expects it.From the quiz crash
    • Name the environment before you trust it. Development and production can look alike, so every script states exactly which one it is about to touch.From a dev check briefly read as production
    • Measure the rendered page. Component tests load no styles. Look at the page at desktop and phone widths, and measure what's inside each box.From the pasted-link overflow

    If I started again, I'd write the guard tests for framework rules on day one and classify every database change from the first file.

    Printivo

    Part 2/16

    E-commerce · Web-to-Print

    Lead Product Designer

    Mudia Imasuen

    View Live Project

    Kaduna, Nigeria
    --:-- (+1 WAT)
    26 images · 13 sections

    Printivo.com launched in 2014 as Nigeria's first web-to-print platform, giving individuals and small businesses easy access to quality prints for stationery and merchandise. It had served over 10,000 customers, but buying from it was harder than it should have been.

    I led the product design of the redesign and rebuild over roughly fourteen weeks, from discovery to beta testing. The goal was simple to state: improve the digital experience for new and existing Printivo customers.

    /01

    Background

    Print changed the world. Ordering it online in Nigeria was still hard.

    Print technology has shaped history since Gutenberg's press in 1440, yet making it easy to reach for individuals and small businesses in Nigeria still had a long way to go, especially for people who won't compromise on quality but have a budget to keep.

    Printivo set out to fix that in 2014. Years and thousands of customers later, the platform needed to catch up with the people using it.

    /02

    Role & approach

    Six people, fourteen weeks, starting with the people behind the counter

    I was the lead product designer, working with a product manager, two front-end and two back-end developers. I started with calls to marketing, customer care and tech to hear what they had been dealing with, good and bad. Marketing gave me a list of frequent customers, and a $10 coupon was approved as a thank-you for anyone who joined an interview.

    /03

    Research

    What customer care knew that the website didn't

    Research ran in two directions: the teams who served customers, and the customers themselves. Tech had the usual bugs, like a print file not attaching to a submitted order. Customer care kept getting calls asking for information that was already on the website, and complaints about wrong search results.

    Customers found the product category pages confusing, and many felt they had to design their own artwork before they could use Printivo at all. So they browsed, called customer care, then sent their print file over WhatsApp. Customer care confirmed this happened often.

    /04

    Contextual inquiry

    Watching people try to buy a business card

    I recruited volunteers on Twitter and watched them use the site while thinking out loud. Each person had to order a business card, a face mask or a mug, three of the most ordered products in our analytics. Almost everyone struggled to complete a simple end-to-end purchase.

    /05

    Goals

    A UX audit turned into four goals

    I compiled every interview into a UX audit and went through it with the Printivo team. Together we set goals for the rebuild that would raise the platform's value, grow the customer base, increase conversion and cut the bounce rate.

    • A more intuitive and delightful design, with quicker task completion
    • A platform that converts leads into purchases
    • Lower churn and bounce rates, with less frustration and confusion
    • A clearer way to find relevant information
    • Faster delivery, which meant building new off-platform infrastructure as well

    /06

    User flow

    Mapped together, then simplified

    We held a series of working sessions in Miro and iterated on the user flow together, tracing every action a customer takes to complete an order. After several rounds we had a detailed flow for the whole platform. The high-level version below covers ordering: categories, products, choosing a product, the design decision, cart and checkout.

    /07

    Home page

    Less noise, search up front, popular products first

    The old landing page was busy and distracting, so I cut the copy back and took a minimal approach. Search became highly visible because customers came looking for something specific, and popular products and categories moved up so people could start an order in one click.

    /08

    Search

    Getting the right results, and a way out when there are none

    Search was one of the loudest complaints, so it got its own round of work with the tech team. Results now show product cards with prices and a quick way in, and searches with no match offer category results and a customer care number instead of a dead end.

    /09

    Category page

    Structure for the information people need to choose

    The old category layout had no clear structure for product information. In the new design every product shows the same details in the same place, with typographic weight used to make material, finishing and delivery times stand out.

    /10

    Product page

    More detail before ordering, and no more blank requests

    I added a product description to every product page, giving customers another look at exactly what they're ordering, along with clear specifications for paper, finish, corners and shipping.

    On the old site, "Order now" opened a modal that asked how you wanted to order, and many requests arrived blank. I moved that step onto its own page, with four clear routes: upload your design, create it yourself, ask the design team, or proceed to checkout.

    /11

    Sign in & checkout

    Keeping the order flowing to the end

    Signing in used to take people to a separate page, then drop them on the home page afterwards. I turned sign-in into a modal, so an order in progress never breaks.

    Checkout was rebuilt to match the new art direction: contact and shipping details on one side, a cost breakdown and a single next step on the other. Payment supports card and bank transfer, and the success page confirms the order and offers to save the card.

    /12

    Design system

    Built alongside the developers, documented as I went

    I worked with the developers on a design system to make building faster and easier. A few pages into high fidelity I already had solid components defined and documented, and the system grew to cover everything the site needed, on desktop and mobile.

    Each component carries its own description in Figma, so developers know what it is for, where it lives and which variants exist.

    /13

    Outcome

    More users, far fewer bounces

    This was my first time in web-to-print. Constant communication with the customer-facing teams, and with customers themselves, made its unusual problems easier to navigate, and I learnt a great deal about UX for e-commerce.

    In the first weeks after launch, sales went up and people spent longer on the site, with a lower bounce rate than we had seen in months.

    Before redesign

    • Average monthly users: 198
    • Monthly new users: 194
    • Page views per session: 0.06
    • Bounce rate: 71.6% to 89.3%

    After redesign

    • Average monthly users grew by 80%
    • Monthly new users grew by 60.08%
    • Average time per visit grew by 2 min 7 s
    • Bounce rate fell to 10.72%

    Speedy Transfer

    Part 3/16

    Fintech · Remittance App

    UI Designer

    Mudia Imasuen

    Kaduna, Nigeria
    --:-- (+1 WAT)
    26 images · 10 sections

    Between 2013 and 2019, Nigeria received $96 billion in diaspora remittances, and their share of GDP kept rising. Much of that money pays for services or supports family, yet sending it still demanded more tech-savvy than most people have, especially after the Central Bank of Nigeria banned popular routes like TransferWise.

    Speedy Transfer set out to make sending money home as easy as paying for coffee with a card. On a three-week timeline I designed the app end to end: onboarding with KYC, instant bank transfers, bill payments, card management and security.

    /01

    Role

    A handed-down PRD, and a few extra hours for research

    I was the UI designer. The deadline was short and a PRD arrived in the first week, but I squeezed in extra hours of research so I had real insight before going straight to prototypes.

    /02

    Research

    Phone calls with friends abroad

    Plenty of my friends had relocated, so I interviewed them by phone and met a few who were back in town. All of them had sent money home at some point, and all of them found it inconvenient, or found it a hassle for the person receiving it.

    In Nigeria bank-to-bank transfers are instant, so jumping through hoops to send cash home feels especially painful. CBN regulation was also making it harder to send money without going to a bank.

    /03

    Constraint

    One question: how might we make it instant?

    What mattered most was that money arrived instantly. If Sani in London wants to pay a light or TV bill in Nigeria, or send money to his brother, he wants it done in minutes. A wallet system would make that possible, but regulation wouldn't allow a digital FX wallet for each person.

    After a series of calls with the team's business analyst we found a way around it. The details are under NDA, but the screens below are the result.

    /04

    User flow

    Five areas, agreed before a single screen

    From the research I drew a user flow to guide the design. This simplified version shows what we agreed on: onboarding, a home dashboard, bank transfers, bill payments, transaction history and a "More" area for profile, cards, support and logout.

    /05

    Onboarding

    From welcome to first transfer in a few steps

    With the north star set I went straight to high fidelity, with an art direction I wanted to try: warm peach panels, deep teal actions, and friendly 3D illustrations that make a money app feel human. Onboarding asks for contact details, verifies the phone number, collects basic location details for KYC and sets a secure password.

    /06

    Bank transfer

    Amount, recipient, card, done

    To send money the user enters an amount in pounds and sees the naira equivalent straight away. They pick a saved recipient or add a new account, choose or add a card, review a full breakdown with the exchange rate and fee, then pay.

    /07

    Bill payment

    Paying for things back home

    Bills follow the same pattern so nothing has to be learnt twice. The user picks a service and category, chooses the amount or bundle, adds details like a TV decoder's smart card number, then pays with a saved card and a transaction PIN.

    /08

    KYC & security

    Verification and account safety

    The "More" area holds profile, cards, support and verification. KYC is a single upload of any valid ID, reviewed within 24 hours. Security settings let users change their password and transaction PIN, with clear rules and friendly confirmations.

    /09

    Handoff

    A component-based system for the developers

    To help the developers collaborate and to make the design easier to scale, I built a component-based design system on a four-column mobile grid, with Encode Sans for headings, Inter for amounts and body copy, and Courier New for card numbers. Handoff ran through Zeplin.

    /10

    Takeaway

    Always talk to users, then tell the business what you heard

    Of all my fintech projects this is my favourite, because it met a pressing need for Nigerians abroad with something as easy as paying for coffee. My key takeaway is how important it is to talk to users, and to communicate those findings to stakeholders, because that is how design insight improves the business model.

    /04

    Invoice, by Bloc

    Part 4/16

    Fintech · Invoice Financing

    Lead Product Designer

    Mudia Imasuen

    Kaduna, Nigeria
    --:-- (+1 WAT)
    33 images · 12 sections

    Contractors and suppliers often win contracts from companies, agencies and governments without the cash to fulfil them before payment arrives. Banks rarely help without a good credit score or a large deposit, so they borrow from friends, family or private lenders.

    Invoice, by Bloc gives contractors quick access to loans, vets them, and helps them supply what the contract asks for. I led the product design from start to finish and prototyped both the contractor and admin platforms.

    /01

    Role

    Two designers, a PM and engineering

    I led product design on a team of two product designers, one product manager and the engineering team, and owned the design of both platforms from start to finish.

    /02

    Kick-off

    Who is this for, and how big is it?

    I started with a few questions. Who is our primary user? What are their goals? How will the business use the product to grow? How large is the scope? With stakeholders and product managers we worked through the Double Diamond to map the problem and decide what to do next.

    /03

    Users

    Three kinds of user, one contract

    Our conversations defined three user categories, each with its own jobs on the platform.

    • The contractor bids for a contract, requests full or partial funding, saves supplier details and sends invoices to the awardee.
    • The Bloc admin reviews companies applying for verification, approves or rejects loan requests, and sees every loan, repayment and invoice.
    • The contract awardee receives the invoice once a loan is approved.

    /04

    Ideation

    Flows before screens

    We drew user flows for every journey to agree the scope of the product and the direction to take. Breaking each journey into touchpoints gave us a rough list of every feature and function the app needed.

    /05

    Visuals

    From paper to a consistent visual system

    After meetings, brainstorms, interviews and research, the sketches moved into Figma. To keep visuals consistent I set rules for typography, components, brand colours and clear calls to action, drawing on the company-wide design system.

    /06

    Contractor: account

    Open a business account in about five minutes

    Creating an account is deliberately short: business name, email and a password. The same credentials sign the contractor back in at any time.

    /07

    Contractor: KYC

    Verification in four clear steps

    A new business lands on a dashboard that asks it to complete KYC before anything else. Verification covers basic business information, a verifiable address, the company's owners, and its legal documents, each as its own step.

    /08

    Contractor: loans

    From request-for-quote to funding

    To apply for a loan, the contractor starts from the request-for-quote document the awardee issued, then adds the client's details, their own contact details, supplier and goods, and the type of financing: full, or partial with Bloc sharing the cost.

    /09

    Contractor: invoices

    Invoices generated once a loan is approved

    Approved loans generate an invoice the contractor can view, download and send to the awardee.

    /10

    Admin platform

    One view of every company, loan and invoice

    The admin dashboard has empty and filled states. Once data arrives it shows loan requests, active and repaid loans, defaulting customers, invoices, total disbursed and total profit.

    From there an admin can open any company to review its KYC details and approve, reject or ask for more information, see every transaction with that company, and work through its loan requests one at a time.

    /11

    Email templates

    Every important action gets an email

    Many actions on the platform trigger an email, from loan applications and verification updates to password resets, so I designed those too.

    /12

    Learnings

    Learning how contracts get funded

    This was unlike any fintech platform I had worked on, and it taught me how invoice funding works. Growing up, my dad was a contractor for the state government, and his stories about funding contracts gave me useful insight.

    The result is a platform that lets suppliers without purchasing power fund the goods a contract requires, at an agreed rate, with full or partial financing depending on the contract.

    /05

    Baiya

    Part 5/16

    Fintech · Cross-border Payments

    Lead Product Designer

    Mudia Imasuen

    Kaduna, Nigeria
    --:-- (+1 WAT)
    16 images · 10 sections

    Nigeria imported US$12.06 billion of goods from China in 2019, but paying Chinese suppliers is slow and strenuous. Central Bank restrictions on commercial banks, like daily withdrawal limits, stretch every trade out.

    Baiya set out to design a better Nigeria-to-China payment system for local traders. I led the product design and delivered three platforms within four weeks.

    /01

    Background

    Trade is booming. Paying for it isn't.

    Much of what Nigeria imports comes from China, and businesses that trade between the two struggle to move money across the border quickly. The goal was to design and improve a Nigeria-to-China payment system for local traders.

    /02

    Role

    Four weeks, led from the first stakeholder call

    I led the product design. My team and I began with calls to the main stakeholders to understand the problem and the business flow, which shaped the information architecture and the customer journey.

    /03

    Insight

    Why traders use middlemen

    The stakeholders and product manager had interviewed executives whose businesses trade often between Nigeria and China. Their feedback came down to three problems.

    • International transfers through traditional banks take at least 5 to 7 working days from start to finish.
    • To avoid the banks, many traders pay middlemen and agents to move money faster.
    • Few banks can send money to Asia, so fees are high.

    /04

    Process

    Lean UX: think, make, check

    Discovery took four days of stakeholder conversations and user research. Then I chose the problems with the most impact and drew the information architecture and flows, sketched screens from low to high fidelity, and shared prototypes for testing and iteration.

    /05

    Platforms

    One product, three platforms

    With stakeholders and product managers we split the solution into three core platforms.

    • The Business platform lets traders send money from Nigeria to China.
    • The Admin platform lets administrators onboard companies and manage the product.
    • The Payout platform lets agents monitor money movements and resolve transfer disputes.

    /06

    Low fidelity

    Sketches, digitised early

    I digitised our sketches before moving to high fidelity. Getting the structure in front of the team early let engineering flag technical issues while they were still cheap to fix.

    /07

    Payout platform

    Watching the money move

    Payout admins sign in, see an overview of recent transactions with totals for businesses, transfers and blocks, and work through tables of transfers and transfer blocks. They can open any transaction, update its status with a note, and manage other payout administrators.

    /08

    Admin platform

    Onboarding and managing businesses

    The admin dashboard gives a quick view of businesses, transfers and wallet requests. Admins can generate reports, view and update any business, and verify new ones.

    /09

    Business platform

    From wallet to confirmed transfer

    A business sees its wallet balance and recent transactions, with a clear way to start a new one. A new transaction takes the sender's details, the recipient's Chinese bank details, and the amount in naira, yuan and dollars. Once authorised with a PIN, the sender gets an account number to pay the naira into.

    /10

    Conclusion

    Lower fees, fewer middlemen

    Baiya lets businesses trade between Nigeria and China efficiently, with the lowest transaction fees and without the hassle of the traditional banking system. Businesses asked to beta test it, and it was being bundled into Bloc's remittance APIs.

    /06

    Gova Pay

    Part 6/16

    Fintech · Mobile Wallet

    Product Design

    Mudia Imasuen

    Kaduna, Nigeria
    --:-- (+1 WAT)
    2 images · 7 sections

    Gova Pay is a mobile wallet for Nigerians, built around one line from its own intro screens: connecting the banked and the unbanked. People send and receive money in a few taps, request money from friends and contacts, buy airtime, and hold a virtual dollar card for paying abroad.

    I designed the core of the app in a dark interface with violet actions and soft 3D illustrations, then mapped every flow screen by screen and annotated each one for the developers who would build it.

    /01

    Onboarding

    Verified before the first naira moves

    The app opens on an animated splash, then four intro cards whose image, text and counter move together. Registration starts with the customer's Bank Verification Number (BVN), which is checked before anything else. A face scan follows, doing two jobs at once: matching the face to the BVN photo, and a liveness check to make sure a real person is holding the phone.

    Only then does the customer create the account itself. The password rules tick off live as they type, a mismatch between the phone number's registered name and the BVN is caught on the spot, and a one-time code confirms the phone before the success screen and the new home dashboard.

    /02

    Returning users

    A PIN instead of a password

    Typing a password every time is the slowest part of opening a money app. So the first successful login ends on a modal asking the customer to set a six-digit PIN, which they create and confirm. From then on, the login screen asks only for that PIN. Signing out lives in the More tab.

    /03

    Sending money

    Three ways to pay someone

    People think of the person they are paying in different ways, so there are three routes. A GovaPay tag is a username, found by search or picked from contacts. A phone number can be typed or imported straight from the address book. A bank account is checked against the account holder's name before anything is sent.

    All three end the same way: a confirmation sheet showing exactly who is being paid and how much, an authentication step, then a receipt.

    /04

    Airtime & bills

    Recharge in four taps

    Payments sit in one grid: airtime, internet, electricity, TV, with betting marked as coming soon. Buying airtime means picking the network, entering a number or tapping a recent payment, and choosing an amount, with the account balance shown underneath. The same confirm, authenticate and done pattern closes it out.

    /05

    Dollar card

    A virtual dollar card for paying abroad

    The virtual dollar card is the feature that makes the wallet useful beyond Nigeria. A customer can get a card, confirm it, view its details, fund it from the wallet and see its transactions. Freezing and deleting are both on the card's own management screen, each with a confirmation and a clear success state.

    /06

    Components

    Seven flows, one set of parts

    The flows above feel like one product because they are assembled from the same few pieces. A customer who has confirmed one transfer already knows how to confirm an airtime top-up or a card top-up, because it is the same sheet followed by the same prompt.

    These are the parts that repeat, pulled out of the screens they live in.

    /07

    Handoff

    A note on every screen

    Every screen in these flows carries a developer's note: what it is for, what it checks, and what should happen next. Some notes explain behaviour a static frame cannot show, like the splash animating every time the app opens, or the intro cards' image, text and counter moving as one component.

    Others raise open questions in the open, where the whole team could answer them: how customers who start on USSD should get into the app, and whether referral codes belonged in this release. Writing them beside the screen, not in a separate document, meant nobody could build one without reading the other.

    Bloc One

    Part 7/16

    Product Design

    Mudia Imasuen

    Kaduna, Nigeria
    --:-- (+1 WAT)
    17 images · 7 sections

    Bloc One is the web dashboard for businesses banking with Bloc, a Nigerian banking platform. From one sidebar a business manages its balances, customers and transactions, sends transfers, issues naira and dollar cards, runs POS terminals, pays bills, and collects money through payment providers and payment links.

    I designed the way a business arrives and finds its feet: sign-up, a getting-started checklist, guided product tours, a partner workspace for SMEDAN, the transactions views, security settings and the emails that go with them.

    /01

    Sign up

    An account in one form

    Creating a Bloc account is a single form: the owner's name, the business name and category, email, country, phone number and a password whose requirements are listed underneath. Verifying the email lands on a simple "You're good to go" screen with one way forward, into the dashboard.

    /02

    Getting started

    A checklist that ends in live mode

    New accounts open in test mode on a Get Started page. A setup guide lists what to do, in order: verify the email, complete KYC, add a payment provider, create a payment link and make a test payment. Beside it sit a demo video and links to help, the API docs and the status page.

    When the last step is done the page changes its message: you're all set, here's how to switch to live mode.

    /03

    Product tours

    Short tours, one per product

    Instead of one long walkthrough, each part of the product introduces itself the first time it is opened. The welcome tour covers navigation and settings in five steps. The cards tour shows naira and dollar business debit cards created in seconds, each assigned to a team member for accountability. Payment links get two steps: fill in the basics, share the link, get paid.

    /04

    SMEDAN

    A workspace for SMEDAN businesses

    Bloc One also ran a workspace for SMEDAN, Nigeria's Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency. A business signed in there is welcomed by name and shown a catalogue of payment products, from Bloc and from partners like Flutterwave, Paystack, Mono, Thepeer, Yoco and Fluidcoins. They can search it or filter by category, then open any product for its details.

    The same workspace handles KYB, know your business. An admin view brings the company's information, its director and every required document into one verified record, and the business sees a clear "you're done" when compliance is complete.

    /05

    Transactions

    Every payment, and a receipt for each

    Transactions are one filterable, searchable table: status, reference, amount, source, method, credit or debit, and date, with an export. Bank transfers, cards, USSD and crypto all land in the same list. Opening a row slides in a detail panel; the full view adds the customer, provider and commission, and produces a receipt that includes the device and location the payment came from.

    /06

    Security

    Two-factor, a scan away

    Settings are split into tabs. Security holds the password change and two-factor authentication, which is set up by scanning a QR code with an authenticator app and entering the code it produces.

    /07

    Emails

    The emails that go with it

    Important moments get an email in the same visual language: a personal welcome from a product specialist, a notice that an account has been flagged and frozen for suspicious activity with a link to support, and the note that it has been unfrozen.

    QuickCash Pay

    Part 8/16

    Fintech · P2P Payments

    Product Design

    Mudia Imasuen

    Kaduna, Nigeria
    --:-- (+1 WAT)
    9 images · 7 sections

    QuickCash Pay LLC is a peer-to-peer money app for the United States, with a simple promise on its first screen: the quickest way to send money within America. People send and request cash by username or phone number, connect cards and bank accounts, and move received funds out instantly or for free.

    I designed the app end to end, in deep green with a lime highlight and a warm, emoji-friendly voice that keeps money tasks feeling light.

    /01

    Onboarding

    A username first, then everything else

    Sign-up starts with the thing people will be paid by: a username, checked for availability as it is typed. Then contact details, a choice of how to receive the one-time code (text, WhatsApp, an authenticator app or email), the code itself, and a password with its rules ticked off live. It ends on a celebration and a button into the dashboard.

    /02

    Sign in

    Login and a gentle reset

    Returning users log in with a username or email. A forgotten password takes one email address, then a clear confirmation that a reset link is on its way.

    /03

    Connect a card

    An empty wallet that tells you what to do

    A new wallet shows $0 and, instead of an empty state, the two things that unlock it: connect a bank account or connect a card. A "why do I need to connect my card?" link answers the question before it is asked, and success offers to connect another.

    /04

    Send cash

    Amount, person, source, PIN

    Sending starts on a big keypad with the wallet balance above it. The sender picks someone by handle or phone number and adds a note, then confirms, choosing whether the money comes from a bank account or a card. The first payment asks them to create a transaction PIN; after that, Face ID or the PIN verifies each one.

    /05

    Request cash

    Asking for money, with the fee shown up front

    Requests mirror sending, so nothing has to be learnt twice. The difference is where the money lands: the requester chooses which card or bank account receives it, and the fee is stated plainly with the amount they will actually get, before they confirm with a fingerprint or PIN.

    /06

    Accounts & cards

    Money sources you can manage

    Account management covers profile, security, app settings, deleting the account and logging out. Bank accounts are added with routing and account numbers, confirmed twice, and each one can be edited, made primary, disconnected or deleted. Deleting is irreversible, so it gets its own confirmation that says exactly that.

    /07

    History

    Pending money, moved when you want it

    Transaction history has two tabs: everything, and funds that are pending because they have not yet been moved out. From there the customer transfers money to a bank account or card, and chooses an instant transfer with a fee or a delayed one for free, with the exact amount they will receive shown before they commit.
